Civilization Is Not Running. It Is Coasting.

Martin Kessler · October 20, 2026

A flywheel does not stop when the engine cuts out. It keeps turning, smoothly and convincingly, on stored rotation. Anyone watching the wheel sees motion and concludes the machine is working. The motion is real. The power behind it is gone.

That is the correct way to read the present moment. The statistics of modern life still describe a functioning civilization: full shelves, lit cities, aircraft on schedule, data moving at light speed. Almost none of that is evidence about the present. It is evidence about the past, arriving late. Complex systems do not fail when their foundations fail. They fail afterward, on a delay set by physics, and the delay is long enough to be mistaken for safety.

Engineers call this lag. The book I have spent two years assembling calls it by its plainer name: residual momentum.

The surplus that built the wheel

Every complex society runs on a surplus: energy left over after the cost of obtaining energy. Agrarian societies ran on a thin one, which is why they stayed small, poor, and local. Industrial civilization ran on the thickest surplus in the history of the species, and it spent that surplus on everything we now call normal: suburbs, universities, antibiotics, aviation, the internet, eight billion people.

The surplus has a measurement. Energy return on investment, EROI, counts the units of energy a society gets back for each unit it spends on extraction. The numbers form a single, unbroken gradient. In 1919, finding oil and gas in the United States returned roughly 1,200 units for every unit spent looking (Guilford et al., 2011). By 2007 the same activity returned about 5 to 1. The cumulative EROI of all fossil fuels peaked near 44 to 1 in the early 1960s and has declined continuously since (Court and Fizaine, 2017). Measured at the point of use, after refining, the global fossil system now delivers about 6 to 1 (Brockway et al., 2019, Nature Energy). Below roughly 5 to 1 lies what energy researchers call the net energy cliff, where the surplus available to run everything else does not decline gradually. It falls off an edge.

None of this is a forecast. It is a ledger of what has already happened, most of it before anyone now arguing about energy policy was born. The best fuel was burned first, because that is what "best" means: shallowest, richest, closest. What remains is deeper, thinner, and more expensive in the only currency that cannot be printed, which is energy itself.

And yet the wheel turns. Flights board. Crops move. This is not a contradiction. It is the lag, doing exactly what lag does.

The store, measured directly

Most of the ledger is inference: ratios, grades, deferred maintenance schedules. Groundwater is the exception. Satellites can weigh it.

The GRACE gravity satellites assessed the planet's 37 largest aquifer systems over a decade (2003–2013). Eight were classified as overstressed, meaning almost no natural replenishment offsets what is pumped out of them (Richey et al., 2015, Water Resources Research). The most overstressed system on Earth, the Arabian Aquifer, supplies more than 60 million people. The Indus Basin aquifer, under northwestern India and Pakistan, ranked second. California's Central Valley aquifer was classed as highly stressed, and the study's conclusion was blunt: the system "is unable to balance the combined impact of groundwater use and drought, either through capture or active management, and is therefore not a resilient system."

The water in these basins is not income. Much of it fell as rain thousands of years ago, and recharge runs on timescales of centuries to millennia while extraction runs on the timescale of a growing season. In central Saudi Arabia, annual withdrawal exceeds recharge by more than six to one, drawn largely from the Saq aquifer: fossil water, mined, with a hard physical endpoint. Northern India is losing roughly 32.5 cubic kilometres of groundwater a year, and pumping, not glacier melt, accounts for most of it. A 2026 GRACE-based reanalysis then found that earlier methods had underestimated depletion by about 45 percent on average. Note the direction of the error. When the instruments were corrected, the store was smaller than the ledger said.

This is residual momentum in its purest available form. The farms are green, the cities are lit, the harvests ship on schedule, and every one of those facts is compatible with the basin underneath them emptying on a schedule already set. The surface reports the spending. Only the satellites report the balance.

Why nothing looks broken

Infrastructure is the flywheel's rim: massive, slow, and built by a richer era. The American Society of Civil Engineers graded United States infrastructure C-minus in 2021, with a ten-year investment gap of $2.59 trillion and spending running at just over half of what maintenance requires. Read that correctly. The system is not being maintained at replacement level. It is being consumed, politely, at a rate slow enough that each individual failure reads as an accident instead of a trend. A bridge closure is news. The arithmetic behind ten thousand bridge closures is a report nobody reads.

The same structure repeats everywhere the book's research looked. Aquifers drawn down over decades recharge over centuries. Ore grades fall on geological schedules while mine development takes sixteen to seventeen years from discovery to production. Debt compounds on quarterly schedules against infrastructure that depreciates over fifty to one hundred years. In every domain, the slow variable was loaded during the high-surplus era, and the slow variable is still responding to inputs from decades ago. The fast variables, prices, elections, quarterly earnings, policy announcements, operate on timescales far too short to change the slow ones. They can only narrate them.

This is why the debate about the future is so consistently wrong in both directions. The optimist points at the turning wheel and calls it an engine. The pessimist predicts the wheel stops tomorrow and is embarrassed when it does not. Both are misreading a lag as an argument. Momentum is not health, and it is not fraud either. It is stored work, being spent. The only real question is the size of the store and the rate of the spend, and those are accounting questions, not ideological ones.

The pipeline is already written

The same arithmetic governs what the next energy system is supposed to be built from. Copper ore grades have fallen roughly 40 percent since 1991 (IEA); across the twentieth century the average grade declined from about 2 percent to about 0.8 percent (Mudd, 2009). Each tonne of metal now requires moving, crushing, and processing far more rock than the tonne before it, which means more energy spent per unit of the material every electrification plan depends on. High prices cannot repeal this. They can only make worse geology worth mining.

Discovery tells the same story from the other end. Of 228 copper deposits found between 1990 and 2021, only 3 were found in the final five years of that window. And a mine takes sixteen to seventeen years on average to travel from discovery to production (IEA). Add those two facts and the conclusion is not a prediction. It is bookkeeping. Even if every announced project proceeds on schedule, supply gaps for copper and lithium persist through 2035, quantified in the IEA's 2025 outlook at roughly 30 percent for copper and 40 percent for lithium. The supply pipeline of the mid-2030s was substantially decided by exploration results from decades ago, and no policy announced this year can add a mine to 2032. The lag here is not a metaphor. It is a lead time, printed in a table.

A civilization that plans in election cycles is structurally incapable of seeing this, because the variables that decide its future were locked in before the current planners took office and will not finish playing out until after they leave. What looks like a choice about the future is usually an argument about the past, conducted by people who do not know it has already happened.

The honest measurement

Strip the narrative away and three quantities describe the situation. First, the surplus ratio, which peaked two generations ago and declines on a geological gradient that no election reverses. Second, the maintenance bill for the built world, which grows every year it is deferred and is currently being paid at roughly half rate. Third, the lag itself: the distance between when a foundation gives way and when the structure notices. That distance is measured in decades, which is precisely why a civilization can be past its point of no return and still feel, from inside, like a going concern.

There is a corollary that rarely gets stated. Response capacity is itself bought with surplus. Rebuilding a bridge, replacing an aquifer's function with desalination, opening a mine in worse rock: every available response is an energy purchase, and the currency is the same surplus whose decline created the problem. Deferral does not merely move the bill. It shrinks the means of paying it while the bill grows. That is the mechanical content of "too late": not a date on a calendar, but a crossing point where the cost of the response exceeds the surplus left to fund it. Past that point, the conversation about solutions continues at full volume. It has simply become ceremonial.

A pilot has a phrase for the most dangerous segment of flight: the region where the aircraft is too low to recover and too committed to stop. The danger is not the fall. The danger is that during most of that segment, nothing feels wrong. The controls respond. The horizon holds. The instruments that would tell the truth are the slow ones, and slow instruments are easy to ignore in a culture that refreshes every few seconds.

Civilization is not running. It is coasting, on the finest momentum any species has ever stored, and coasting feels exactly like flying until the altitude runs out. The mature response is not panic and it is not denial. It is to read the slow instruments, state the ledger plainly, and stop mistaking the smoothness of the wheel for the presence of an engine.
