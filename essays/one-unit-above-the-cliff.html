One Unit Above the Cliff

Martin Kessler · October 30, 2026

Stand at a fuel pump and watch the counter. The number that matters is not the price. It is a ratio, and it runs backward from the nozzle. Of every six units of energy delivered as finished fuel (gasoline, diesel, jet fuel, heating oil), one unit was consumed by the process of delivering it: burned in the drilling rigs, the pumps, the pipelines, the tankers, and the refining that turned crude into something an engine can use. The other five units are the world's actual energy income. Everything that is not the energy industry runs on them.

The ratio has a name, energy return on investment, and at the point where energy actually enters the economy, the global average for fossil fuels is approximately six to one. The estimate comes from the most complete final-stage accounting available (Brockway et al., 2019, Nature Energy), built from International Energy Agency and Exiobase data. At that accounting boundary, fossil fuels return about what solar photovoltaics return. The same study records where the floor sits. Below a return of roughly five to one, the authors warn, the system approaches a net energy cliff, past which the surplus available to society contracts precipitously.

Six, against five. This essay is an audit of the one unit in between: what it is made of, what it currently pays for, and the manner in which its disappearance will be reported as a series of unrelated events.

Counted at the wellhead

The numbers usually quoted for fossil fuels are larger, and they are not lies. They are measurements taken at a flattering point in the chain.

At the point of extraction, the historical record is genuinely spectacular. The energy return on finding oil and gas in the United States stood at roughly 1,200 to one in 1919: a barrel's worth of energy spent searching returned twelve hundred barrels found (Guilford et al., 2011, Sustainability). Global oil production reached its peak return at about 50 to one; gas peaked at about 150 to one; and the cumulative return on all fossil fuels peaked at approximately 44 to one in the early 1960s (Court and Fizaine, 2017, Ecological Economics). Those were the decades in which the modern world's infrastructure was capitalized, and the capitalization was easy because the surplus was enormous. At 44 to one, the energy sector consumed about a fiftieth of the gross flow to run itself. The rest was available for everything else, including waste on a scale no previous civilization could have afforded.

The wellhead numbers have been falling ever since, along a gradient set by geology: the highest-grade deposits are extracted first, and every subsequent tranche is deeper, thinner, more dispersed, or dirtier than the one before it. United States production-stage returns fell from about 20 to one in the mid-twentieth century to roughly 11 to one by the late 2000s, and the finding ratio completed its journey from 1,200 to one down to about 5 to one by 2007. Coal is the lone exception, the one fossil fuel whose extraction return has not yet peaked, and its exception changes nothing in the delivered average.

The decline is also occasionally disguised in the data. When drilling effort contracts, measured returns rise against the trend, as they did from the mid-1980s into the early 2000s, because the industry is harvesting earlier work rather than finding new energy cheaply. The disguise does not change the gradient. It changes the date on which the gradient is admitted.

But the deeper correction is not the trend. It is the location of the measurement. Society does not burn crude oil at the wellhead. It burns finished fuel, and making finished fuel costs energy that the extraction figures never book. Counted at the finished-fuel stage, the point where energy enters the economy and becomes available to do work, the global fossil average is approximately six to one, and it fell by 23 percent in the sixteen years from 1995 to 2011 alone. The spectacular numbers and the modest number are both accurate. They are answers to different questions, and only one of the questions is the one a civilization has to answer.

The arithmetic of the last unit

The reason a threshold exists at all, rather than a smooth decline in comfort, is the shape of a fraction.

Net energy is what remains after the energy sector has fed itself, and its share of gross output is fixed by a simple expression: at a return of E to one, the net share is (E − 1)/E. At 44 to one, the net share is 97.7 percent. At 20 to one, it is 95 percent. At 6 to one, it is 83.3 percent. At 5 to one, it is 80 percent. Read as a sequence of losses, the fall from 44 to 6 cost the system about fourteen points of net share, spread across six decades. The fall from 6 to 5, a single unit, costs 3.3 points. A one-unit decline at the top of the historical range, from 44 to 43, costs 0.05 points. The last unit above the cliff is therefore worth more than sixty of the units spent on the way down from the peak. Each step downward costs more than the step before it, and the steps are not slowing.

This is what the word cliff is doing in the literature. It is not a metaphor for drama. It is a description of a curve whose slope steepens as the margin narrows, so that a steady decline in the ratio produces an accelerating decline in the surplus. Brockway and his co-authors warn that below roughly five to one the contraction in net energy becomes rapid, that fuel availability can be restricted in rapid and unexpected ways, and that such shortages have historically correlated with economic instability. A system one unit above that line does not have a small problem. It has a buffer that an established trend can consume quickly: the decline measured between 1995 and 2011, merely continued, crosses the remaining interval well inside a generation.

One qualification belongs in the ledger, because the thresholds in this literature are not measured at the same fence. Estimates of the minimum return a society requires range from about 3 to one at the mine mouth (Hall and colleagues), to roughly 7 to one for a developed industrial economy (Weißbach et al., 2013, Energy), to around 11 to one in estimates tied to continued economic growth of the American type (Fizaine and Court). The finished-fuel figure of six to one is an economy-wide average at the point of use, not an electricity-sector figure, and it cannot be laid directly over any single threshold. What can be said without stretching any source is narrower, and sufficient: the delivered margin now sits inside the band where every research tradition in this field locates the onset of binding constraint, and it is traveling downward through that band.

What the surplus was paying for

The five net units are not an abstraction. They are the food system, the freight network, the hospital, the school, and the state, all of it running on the difference between what energy costs to obtain and what it delivers. When the ratio falls, the energy sector's claim on gross output rises mechanically. At six to one, one unit in six of all energy produced is consumed in producing energy. The remainder is what every other activity must be built from, including the activity of building whatever comes next.

That last entry is the one the audit has to read twice, because the proposed remedy is itself an energy expenditure, and it is front-loaded. The input energy for wind and solar generation is paid almost entirely in advance, in manufacturing and installation, where fossil systems pay as they go. A rapid buildout therefore draws the surplus down before it adds to it: during the construction phase, the transition is an energy sink, competing with every other use of a thinning margin. Storage deepens the draw. Unbuffered solar photovoltaics in mid-latitudes return somewhere between about 3.5 and 5.9 to one, depending on the method used (Weißbach et al., 2013; Raugei et al., 2012; Brandt et al., 2013). Add the overbuild and pumped storage required to make that power dispatchable, and the return is more than halved. At very high penetration, the return on the marginal unit of solar capacity with battery storage falls by as much as 98.8 percent (Palmer, 2017). None of this is an argument about whether the buildout should occur. It is a statement about what the buildout costs, denominated in the same units as everything else, at the moment when the margin available to pay is the narrowest of the industrial era.

The material ledger compounds the energy one. An electric car requires roughly six times the minerals of a conventional car. An onshore wind farm requires roughly nine times the minerals of a gas-fired plant of comparable output (IEA). And the minerals are subject to the same decay as the fuels: average copper ore grades have fallen about 40 percent since 1991, so each tonne of metal arrives carrying a larger energy cost than the tonne before it, charged against the same six-to-one account.

What a civilization can no longer afford, at this margin, is simultaneity. It cannot run the existing system at full output, rebuild the energy base underneath it, and keep the discretionary overhead that the 44-to-one era made normal, all out of five net units, when the rebuild and the overhead are growing and the five are shrinking. Something in that list is already being quietly de-funded. The de-funding appears in no budget. It appears as deferred maintenance, as projects that no longer pencil out, as resilience converted to throughput. The accounts balance every year. They balance by spending the buffer.

The lag

Here is the part of the ledger that matters, and it is a statement about timing, not about blame.

The margin did not narrow last year. Cumulative fossil returns peaked in the early 1960s. The finished-fuel ratio's measured fall, 23 percent in sixteen years, was complete by 2011 and published in 2019. The system has been operating inside the narrowing for longer than most of the institutions now managing it have existed in their present form, and it has registered the change almost exclusively as price: as fuel costs, subsidy arguments, and inflation episodes, each filed under its own heading, none filed under the ratio that connects them. Prices are the fast instruments. The ratio is a slow one, and slow instruments are read last.

That ordering is the lag. The crossing, when it comes, will not be announced as a crossing. It will be reported as a sequence of shortages, price instabilities, and local failures, each with a local explanation, in the manner the 2019 study describes: rapid, unexpected, and correlated with economic instability. The explanations will be accurate at their own level, the way a dry well's explanation is accurate. What they will omit is the shared arithmetic underneath: a surplus that stood at 44 to one when the modern world was built, that measures six to one at the point of use on the best available accounting, and whose floor, on that same accounting, is five.

The reader was promised a number rather than a mood, so the essay ends with the number. Six to one is what the fuel in the tank returns. Five to one is where the surplus falls off its curve. Civilization is currently operating in the interval between those two figures, and the interval is one unit wide.
