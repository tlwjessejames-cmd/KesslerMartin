The Culture War Is a Symptom. The Disease Is Consciousness.

Martin Kessler · October 18, 2026

Consider the sheer thermal output of modern political life. Not the policies — the temperature. The white-hot fury expended on symbolic micro-battles: the casting of a commercial, the wording of a plaque, a comedian's decade-old joke exhumed and put on trial. Millions of hours of human attention, the most valuable commodity in the economy, burned daily on conflicts that will not change a single material condition of anyone's life.

And then consider the animal burning it. An apex predator with unprecedented technological mastery: fed, sheltered, medically maintained, statistically safer than any generation in history, and profoundly, chronically miserable. The antidepressant statistics read like a livestock report. The loneliness statistics read like a siege diary.

Something is wrong with this picture, and it is not a policy failure. It is older and stranger than politics.

The organ that grew too large

In 1933, the Norwegian philosopher Peter Wessel Zapffe published an essay called "The Last Messiah." It opens with an image: early humans, newly conscious, sitting by the waterhole after the hunt, and waking up to the cosmic joke. They had been given an instrument that overshot its purpose.

Zapffe's argument was biological, not mystical. Evolution, he said, had aimed too high. It equipped a survival machine with a consciousness far in excess of what survival required: an intellect that could not only track game and evade predators, but could contemplate its own death, the indifference of the universe, and the total absence of inherent meaning. Consciousness, in this telling, is not a divine gift. It is a design flaw. A mis-evolution: an organ that grew too large for the skull that holds it.

A deer does not lie awake considering its mortality. A human does, and then must somehow get up and go to work in the morning. Zapffe's question, the one that animates everything he wrote, was simple: why hasn't the species gone extinct from sheer existential horror? Why no great epidemics of madness?

His answer: because we cheat. Raw, unfiltered consciousness is unlivable, so the species evolved four psychological defense mechanisms: four ways of limiting consciousness so that life can continue. He named them plainly:

Isolation. The collective, tacit agreement to pretend the condition doesn't exist. We compartmentalize: death goes in one room, meaninglessness in another, and polite conversation stays in the hallway between them.

Anchoring. We fix meaning to absolute constructs (God, the nation, the party, the cause, morality itself) and build a wall against the abyss out of them. The anchor doesn't need to be true. It needs to be fixed.

Distraction. We enthrall the mind with endless external stimuli: entertainment, status competition, novelty, motion. Keep the surplus cognition busy and it won't turn inward.

Sublimation. We transform the horror into art, architecture, philosophy, the rarest escape valve, available to almost no one.

For most of human history, these four walls held. Tradition, religion, community, and shared local reality did the anchoring; ritual and labor did the distracting; and isolation was maintained by the simple fact that most people never had time to think.

Then the walls came down.

When the anchors rot

The twentieth century dismantled the old anchors with industrial efficiency. Shared religion thinned into private spirituality and then into nothing. Local community dissolved into suburbs and screens. The grand narratives — progress, nation, faith — were either discredited or simply outlived. Then the twenty-first century finished the job: the migration from physical third spaces to digital feeds stripped away the last passive insulation, the background hum of merely being around other people that had quietly done half of Isolation's work for it. What remained was the most over-equipped consciousness in biological history standing exposed to the full draft of the abyss, with nowhere to fasten itself.

An animal in that condition does not calmly deliberate. It panics. And a panicking mind does what minds do: it grabs for the nearest fixed object.

This is the culture war, seen from Zapffe's altitude. It is not a battle of rational actors disagreeing about tax rates. It is a mass psychological immune response: millions of surplus minds clutching at political identities the way a drowning person clutches wreckage. The ideology doesn't need to be coherent. It needs to be absolute. It tells you who you are, who the enemy is, and what the stakes are, and in doing so it spares you from the one confrontation the overgrown brain cannot survive: silence.

Look at the structure of the thing without partisan goggles and the symmetry is unmistakable. Both tribes run identical machinery. Both maintain heresy lists. Both perform public rituals of purification. Both treat deviation as contamination and apostates as traitors. Both have their sacred texts, their clergy, their excommunications. The content differs; the function is the same function. It is anchoring, Zapffe's second mechanism, running in overdrive, because the old anchors rotted and the brain cannot tolerate the free fall.

Ernest Becker saw the same machinery from a different angle. In The Denial of Death (1973), he argued that human beings sustain themselves through "immortality projects," cultural enterprises including political movements and ideological crusades, that promise the individual a share in something deathless. The movement will outlive you; therefore, in the only way that matters, so will you. A culture war is an immortality project with a comments section. It offers what the abyss cannot: significance.

The proxy battlefield

Zapffe had a phrase for our condition: spiritual unemployment. A mind built for tracking game across savannas, for reading weather and kin and terrain, a vast cognitive surplus with no biologically meaningful outlet, now sits in a climate-controlled room staring at a rectangle. The machinery is still running. It still needs a hunt.

The culture war is the hunt, restaged as farce. It provides what the padded modern existence structurally cannot: stakes, enemies, adrenaline, martyrdom. The outrage cycle delivers a perfect neurochemical wage: the hot rush of righteous anger, the warm solidarity of the in-group pile-on, the narcotic certainty that you are on the right side of the only fight that matters. It is pseudo-purpose, precision-engineered. Nobody's material life improves because a stranger was ratioed. But the brain, for one bright hour, felt employed.

And here the design flaw meets the business model. The attention economy did not create the surplus mind, but it industrialized its exploitation. The algorithms discovered, through pure reinforcement learning, what Zapffe described philosophically: that nothing engages the overgrown brain like a threat to its anchor. Outrage is engagement. Engagement is revenue. So the platforms built the most efficient distraction-and-anchoring engines in history: infinite scroll as Zapffe's third mechanism, monetized; the tribe as his second, gamified. Every swipe tightens the anchor and deepens the trench, and the trench feels like meaning.

Thomas Ligotti, the horror writer who carried Zapffe's thesis further than anyone, called consciousness a biological glitch that should never have happened (The Conspiracy Against the Human Race). Read the average political timeline through Ligotti's lens and it stops looking like discourse at all. It looks like a glitch, screaming. A species-wide error message, refreshed every few seconds, forever.

The irony that proves him right

Here is the terminal irony, and it is pure Zapffe: every attempt to solve the cultural divide through the divide's own machinery only deepens it. More outrage to cure outrage. More purity to cure purity spirals. More anchoring to fix the failure of anchors. Each escalation feels like treatment and functions as infection, because the disease was never disagreement. The disease is a consciousness too large for its container, and no political victory can shrink it.

This is why the fever never breaks no matter who wins. Elections are fought, mandates are claimed, the other tribe is humiliated, and within weeks the temperature is back, because the temperature was never about the election. It was about the abyss, and the abyss doesn't hold elections.

Zapffe's last messiah, in the 1933 essay, comes not with salvation but with a single command: know yourselves — be infertile, and let the earth be silent after ye. It was never going to happen, and Zapffe knew it. The species will not lay down its consciousness. It will do what it has always done: build new walls from whatever material is at hand, and right now the material is politics, and the walls are on fire, and everyone is inside them insisting the smoke means they're winning.

The culture war will not be resolved. It cannot be, because it was never the problem. It is what a mis-evolved animal does with the hours, manufacturing meaning out of mutual hatred, because the alternative is sitting quietly by the waterhole and hearing, for the first time in full, the joke.
